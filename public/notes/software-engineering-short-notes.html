1. Software engineering: meaning, need and characteristics

Software engineering is the systematic, disciplined and measurable application of engineering principles to the development, operation, testing and maintenance of software. Its objective is to produce reliable, maintainable and usable software within agreed cost and time limits.

  • Software product: programs, data structures and associated documentation/configuration information.
  • Software process: the organized activities used to specify, design, build, test, deliver and maintain a software product.
  • Software crisis: difficulties such as late delivery, cost overruns, unreliable products, poor maintainability and failure to satisfy user requirements.
  • Good software attributes: correctness, reliability, usability, efficiency, security, maintainability, portability and reusability.

Software engineering manages complexity through requirements discipline, modular design, reviews, testing, configuration control, documentation and project planning.

2. SDLC and software process activities

Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC) is the structured sequence of activities through which software is planned, specified, designed, implemented, verified, deployed and maintained. The exact names and order of phases can vary by process model.

PhaseMain activitiesTypical deliverable
Planning and feasibilityDefine problem, scope, objectives, cost, schedule and risksProject plan / feasibility report
Requirements analysisCollect, analyse, prioritize and validate stakeholder needsSRS
DesignChoose architecture; design modules, data, interfaces and algorithmsArchitecture and detailed design
ImplementationTranslate design into source code and perform code-level checksProgram modules
TestingDetect defects and verify required behaviourTest cases and test reports
DeploymentInstall, configure, migrate data, release and train usersOperational release
MaintenanceCorrect faults, adapt to change and improve the productUpdated versions and fixes

Umbrella activities continue across the lifecycle: project tracking, risk management, software quality assurance, technical reviews, measurement, configuration management and documentation.

Exam distinction: SDLC describes the lifecycle activities; a process model describes how those activities are organized and iterated.

3. Feasibility study and requirements engineering

A feasibility study determines whether a proposed system is practical and worth undertaking before major development effort is committed. A common memory aid is TELOS.

FeasibilityQuestion asked
TechnicalAre technology, infrastructure and skills available?
EconomicDo expected benefits justify development and operating costs?
LegalDoes the solution comply with laws, licences, contracts and regulations?
OperationalWill the system work in the organization and be accepted by users?
ScheduleCan the system be delivered within the required time?

Requirements engineering is the process of discovering, analysing, documenting, validating and managing stakeholder needs. Typical steps are elicitation, analysis/negotiation, specification, validation and change management.

  • Functional requirement: states a service or behaviour the system must provide, such as calculating salary or generating an invoice.
  • Non-functional requirement: states a quality attribute or constraint, such as response time, availability, security, usability, portability or capacity.
  • Constraint: a restriction on design or implementation, such as a required platform, standard or technology.

SRS (Software Requirements Specification) is the formal document describing what the system must do, its interfaces, constraints and quality requirements. It is a basis for design, estimation, testing, acceptance and change control.

A good SRS is correct, complete, unambiguous, consistent, verifiable/testable, feasible, traceable, modifiable and prioritized. Requirements traceability links each requirement to its source, design elements, implementation and test cases.

4. Analysis and design tools: DFD, flowchart and structured design

ToolPurpose / exam distinction
Data Flow Diagram (DFD)Shows how data moves between external entities, processes and data stores; it is not primarily a control-flow chart.
FlowchartShows the sequence of operations and decision branches in an algorithm or process.
Decision tableLists combinations of conditions and the action for each combination; useful for complex business rules.
Decision treeRepresents decisions and possible outcomes as branches.
Structure chartShows hierarchical decomposition into modules and their calling relationships.
PseudocodeLanguage-independent, structured description of algorithm steps.

Common DFD symbols: an external entity is commonly shown as a rectangle; a process as a circle or rounded rectangle; a data store as two parallel lines or an open-ended rectangle; and a data flow as a labelled arrow. Notation can vary between conventions.

  • Context diagram: the highest-level view, representing the whole system as one process and showing its interaction with external entities.
  • Level-0 DFD: decomposes the system into its major processes. Some examination material uses “context diagram” and “level-0 DFD” inconsistently; follow the convention specified in the question.
  • Balancing: inputs and outputs crossing a parent process boundary should be preserved when that process is decomposed into a lower-level DFD.

Flowchart symbols: oval/terminator = start or end; rectangle = process; parallelogram = input/output; diamond = decision; arrow = flow line; connector = continuation. A flowchart is useful for visualizing control logic, while a DFD focuses on data movement.

Modularity, cohesion and coupling

Modularity divides a large system into smaller components with clear responsibilities and interfaces. It makes development, testing, replacement and maintenance easier.

Cohesion measures how closely the responsibilities inside one module are related. Higher cohesion is generally desirable. Common order from weakest to strongest is: coincidental, logical, temporal, procedural, communicational, sequential and functional cohesion.

Coupling measures dependency between modules. Lower coupling is generally desirable. Common order from strongest/worst to weakest/best is: content, common, external, control, stamp and data coupling. Exact naming/order can vary slightly across textbooks.

Design principle: aim for high cohesion within modules and low coupling between modules. Avoid modules that directly depend on another module's internal implementation.

  • Abstraction: focuses on essential features while hiding unnecessary detail.
  • Information hiding: keeps internal decisions/data private behind a defined interface.
  • Top-down design: starts with the whole problem and repeatedly decomposes it into smaller modules.
  • Design pattern: a reusable, named solution structure for a recurring design problem; it is not a ready-made complete program.
  • Reverse engineering: analyses an existing system or code to recover higher-level design, structure or requirements.
  • CASE tools: Computer-Aided Software Engineering tools support activities such as modelling, design, coding, testing, documentation and maintenance.

5. Software process models

ModelCore ideaBest fit / limitation
Waterfall / Linear SequentialPhases proceed largely in sequence; each phase produces outputs for the next.Stable, well-understood requirements. Changes late in the process are costly; working software and feedback arrive late.
V-ModelDevelopment phases are paired with corresponding test levels.Emphasizes early test planning and traceability; less flexible for changing requirements.
PrototypingBuild an early model to clarify requirements and obtain user feedback.Useful when requirements are unclear. A throwaway prototype is discarded; an evolutionary prototype is refined.
RADRapid Application Development uses time-boxing, prototyping, reusable components and user participation.Suitable for modular systems with active user involvement; depends on skilled teams and rapid feedback.
IncrementalDelivers the system in a sequence of usable increments.Early value and feedback; requires a sound overall architecture and prioritization.
IterativeRepeatedly revises and improves a solution through cycles.Useful when learning and refinement are expected; iteration alone does not define a particular release strategy.
SpiralRisk-driven cycles involving objectives, risk analysis, development/validation and planning.Useful for large, complex or high-risk projects; risk analysis adds cost and expertise requirements.
AgileShort iterations, frequent delivery, collaboration, customer feedback and adaptation.Useful when requirements evolve. Agile is a family of approaches, not one single model.

RAD phases commonly tested: business modelling, data modelling, process modelling, application generation and testing/turnover. The exact phase labels may vary by RAD description.

Scrum basics: a lightweight Agile framework. Accountabilities are Product Owner, Scrum Master and Developers. Work is organized in Sprints; common artifacts are Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog and Increment. Agile is broader than Scrum.

Repeated PYQ cues: Linear Sequential = Waterfall; Spiral = risk-driven; RAD = rapid/high-speed development; evolving requirements generally conflict with a rigid linear model.

6. Software testing and test design

Software testing executes or evaluates software to find defects and provide evidence about quality and conformance. Testing can reveal defects; it cannot prove that a non-trivial program is completely defect-free.

Testing levelWhat is tested?
Unit testingOne function, class or module, usually by the developer.
Integration testingInterfaces and interactions between combined modules.
System testingThe complete integrated system against system requirements.
Acceptance testingWhether the system meets agreed acceptance criteria and user/business needs.
ApproachMeaning
Black-box testingTests external behaviour using inputs and expected outputs without relying on internal code structure; also called functional/specification-based testing in many exam contexts.
White-box testingTests internal program structure, statements, branches, paths and conditions; also called structural or glass-box testing.
Grey-box testingTests with partial knowledge of internal design or implementation.
  • Equivalence partitioning: divides input values into classes expected to behave similarly; select representative values from each class.
  • Boundary Value Analysis (BVA): focuses on values at and near input limits, because defects often occur at boundaries. For a valid range 1–4000, useful boundary tests include 0, 1, 2, 3999, 4000 and 4001, subject to the specification.
  • Decision-table testing: tests combinations of conditions and their corresponding actions.
  • Statement coverage: percentage of executable statements run. Branch coverage: percentage of decision outcomes exercised. Branch coverage is generally stronger than statement coverage.
  • Regression testing: reruns relevant tests after a change to detect unintended effects.
  • Recovery testing: checks whether the system can recover after failures, crashes or interrupted operations.
  • Stress testing: evaluates behaviour under extreme or beyond-normal workloads. Performance testing: measures response time, throughput and resource use.
  • Security testing: checks confidentiality, integrity, authentication, authorization and resistance to attacks.

7. Verification, validation and quality assurance

Verification asks, “Are we building the product right?” It checks whether work products conform to specifications, often through reviews, inspections, walkthroughs and static analysis.

Validation asks, “Are we building the right product?” It checks whether the implemented system satisfies user needs and intended use, commonly through execution-based testing and acceptance activities.

  • Quality Assurance (QA): process-oriented and preventive; establishes standards, methods, audits and process improvement to provide confidence that quality requirements will be met.
  • Quality Control (QC): product-oriented; detects defects through inspection, measurement and testing.
  • SQA: Software Quality Assurance; planned activities that ensure software processes and products conform to requirements and standards.
  • Software metrics: quantitative measures of product, process or project attributes. Direct measures include size, execution time and defect count; indirect measures include maintainability, functionality or quality indicators.

Reliability: probability that software operates without failure for a specified time in a specified environment. MTTF is Mean Time To Failure; MTTR is Mean Time To Repair/Restore; ROCOF is Rate of Occurrence of Failures. For a repairable system under a simple steady-state assumption, availability is approximately MTTF / (MTTF + MTTR).

8. Software maintenance and configuration management

Maintenance typePurposeExample
CorrectiveFix faults discovered after delivery.Correct a calculation bug.
AdaptiveKeep software usable in a changed environment.Update for a new operating system or regulation.
PerfectiveImprove performance, usability or functionality.Add a requested report or optimize a slow query.
PreventiveReduce future faults and maintenance effort.Refactor fragile code and improve tests.

Software configuration management (SCM) identifies configuration items, controls and records changes, maintains versions and baselines, and supports configuration audits. It prevents uncontrolled modifications and helps reproduce a release.

Patch: a small update intended to fix a defect, close a vulnerability or make a limited change. A software update may include a broader set of fixes or improvements. Documentation includes requirements, design, code comments where useful, test records, user manuals and maintenance information.

9. Software project planning and risk management

Software project management plans and controls scope, effort, cost, schedule, people, quality, communication and risk. Estimation is uncertain and should be revised as information improves.

  • Work Breakdown Structure (WBS): decomposes project scope into manageable deliverables and work packages.
  • Gantt chart: bar chart showing activities against calendar time, duration and progress.
  • PERT chart: network representation of activities/events and dependencies; useful when activity durations are uncertain.
  • PERT three-time estimate: expected duration = (Optimistic + 4 × Most likely + Pessimistic) / 6. This is an estimate, not a guaranteed completion time.
  • Critical path: longest-duration path through a project network; a delay on a critical activity can delay the project unless schedule recovery is possible.
  • Risk management: identify risks, estimate probability and impact, prioritize, plan responses and monitor them. Common responses include avoidance, mitigation, transfer and acceptance.
  • Estimation methods: expert judgement, analogy, decomposition, size-based estimation and algorithmic models such as COCOMO. A technique estimates effort/cost; it is not itself a scheduling chart.
