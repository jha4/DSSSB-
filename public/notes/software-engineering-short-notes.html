Software Engineering — Complete Notes

Coverage: SDLC, feasibility, requirements, SRS, design models, DFD, testing, verification/validation, maintenance, software quality and project management.

1. Software engineering and SDLC

Software engineering applies systematic, disciplined and measurable methods to software development, operation and maintenance. It aims to deliver software that meets requirements within quality, time and cost constraints.

SDLC phaseMain work / deliverable
Planning and feasibilityScope, schedule, cost, risks and feasibility decision
RequirementsElicitation, analysis, specification and validation
DesignArchitecture, modules, data and interface design
ImplementationCoding and unit-level checks
TestingFind defects and verify required behaviour
DeploymentRelease, installation, migration and training
MaintenanceCorrect, adapt, improve and prevent faults

2. Feasibility and requirements

  • Technical feasibility: technology and skills exist. Economic: benefits justify costs. Operational: solution fits the organization. Schedule: delivery is realistic. Legal: requirements comply with laws and contracts.
  • Functional requirement: what the system must do. Non-functional requirement: quality or constraint such as performance, security, usability, reliability or availability.
  • SRS (Software Requirements Specification): agreed, testable description of requirements, interfaces, constraints and quality attributes. It should be clear, complete, consistent, traceable and verifiable.

3. Analysis and design tools

  • DFD: models movement of data between processes, data stores and external entities. It does not primarily describe control flow.
  • Flowchart: shows sequence and branching of logic. Decision table: tabulates condition combinations and actions. Decision tree: branches on conditions. Structure chart: shows module hierarchy and calling relationships.
  • Modularity: divides a system into manageable components. Cohesion: how strongly a module's tasks belong together; higher is generally better. Coupling: dependency between modules; lower is generally better.

4. Verification, validation and testing

Verification: are we building the product right? It checks work products against specifications. Validation: are we building the right product? It checks whether the delivered system meets user needs.

Test level/typePurpose
UnitTests an individual function/module
IntegrationTests interactions between components
SystemTests the complete integrated system
AcceptanceChecks acceptance criteria with users/customer
RegressionChecks changes have not broken existing behaviour
Black-boxTests externally visible input/output behaviour
White-boxTests internal logic, paths and structure

5. Software process models

  • Waterfall: sequential phases; most suitable when requirements are stable and well understood.
  • Prototyping: builds an early model to clarify requirements and obtain feedback; distinguish throwaway and evolutionary prototypes.
  • Spiral: iterative, risk-driven cycles; each loop includes objectives, risk analysis, development/verification and planning.
  • Agile: iterative delivery, frequent feedback, collaboration and adaptation. Scrum is a framework; Agile is broader than Scrum.

6. Maintenance, quality and project management

  • Corrective maintenance: fixes defects. Adaptive: responds to environment changes. Perfective: improves performance/functionality. Preventive: reduces future maintenance risk.
  • Quality assurance: process-oriented prevention and confidence. Quality control: product-oriented inspection/testing.
  • Project planning: estimates effort, cost, schedule, people and dependencies. Risk management identifies, analyzes, responds to and monitors uncertainty.

7. Exam traps

  • DFD models data movement; flowchart models procedural control flow.
  • Verification is conformance to specification; validation is fitness for user needs.
  • High cohesion and low coupling are desirable. Spiral is risk-driven; Waterfall is sequential.
