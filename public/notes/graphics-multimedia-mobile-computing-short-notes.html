Computer Graphics, Multimedia and Mobile Computing — Complete Notes

Coverage: Display concepts, raster/vector graphics, colour models, 2D transformations, clipping, multimedia sampling/compression and mobile technologies.

1. Computer graphics fundamentals

  • Pixel: smallest addressable picture element in a raster image/display. Resolution: number of pixels, often width × height. Aspect ratio: ratio of width to height.
  • Refresh rate: screen refreshes per second (Hz). Persistence: time a phosphor/display element continues emitting light after excitation; classic CRT terminology.
  • Raster graphics: image represented as a grid of pixels; scaling can reveal pixelation. Vector graphics: image represented using geometric primitives and paths; scales cleanly but may require rasterization.
  • CRT: electron beam excites phosphor on a screen. Modern displays commonly use LCD/LED or OLED technologies.

2. Colour models

  • RGB: additive colour model used by displays; red, green and blue light combine. Full intensity of all three gives white in the ideal additive model.
  • CMY/CMYK: subtractive model used in printing; cyan, magenta and yellow inks absorb components of light, with black often added for practical printing.

3. 2D transformations

Using homogeneous coordinates, translation, scaling and rotation can be represented by matrices and combined through multiplication. Order matters: transformations are generally not commutative.

  • Translation: shifts coordinates by (tx, ty).
  • Scaling: multiplies coordinates by scale factors (sx, sy); unequal factors distort proportions.
  • Rotation: rotates about an origin or pivot; in standard Cartesian coordinates a positive angle is counter-clockwise.
  • Reflection: mirrors across an axis/line. Shear: slants a shape by shifting one coordinate in proportion to another.

4. Viewing, clipping and modelling

  • Viewing pipeline: transforms objects from model/world coordinates to viewing/device coordinates and maps them to a viewport.
  • Clipping: removes portions outside a viewing region. Cohen–Sutherland and Liang–Barsky are line-clipping algorithms.
  • Curves: Bezier curves are controlled by control points; B-splines provide piecewise polynomial representations.
  • Modelling: represents objects using wireframe, surface or solid models.

5. Multimedia: image, audio and video

  • Sampling: measures a continuous signal at discrete time intervals. Higher sampling rate captures higher frequencies; Nyquist requires sampling frequency greater than twice the highest signal frequency for ideal band-limited reconstruction.
  • Quantization: maps sample amplitudes to finite discrete levels. Bit depth b permits 2^b levels.
  • Compression: lossless preserves exact data; lossy discards information to reduce size. JPEG is commonly lossy; PNG is lossless; MPEG standards cover audio/video compression families.
  • Frame rate: frames per second. Bit rate depends on resolution, frame rate, colour depth and compression.

7. Exam traps

  • RGB is additive; CMY is subtractive.
  • Sampling is time discretization; quantization is amplitude discretization.
  • Resolution counts pixels; refresh rate is measured in hertz.
  • GPRS and EDGE enhance GSM; LTE is associated with 4G broadband.

Curves, surfaces and solid modelling

  • Curves: represent smooth paths or boundaries. Parametric forms express coordinates as functions of a parameter; Bezier curves are controlled by control points and have the endpoint/tangent behaviour determined by their control polygon.
  • Surfaces: extend curve concepts to two parameters and describe 3D object boundaries. Common introductory representations include polygon meshes and parametric surfaces such as Bezier or spline surfaces.
  • Solid modelling: represents a complete 3D object, not only its visible surface. Boundary representation (B-rep) stores faces, edges and vertices; constructive solid geometry (CSG) combines primitives using union, intersection and difference; a spatial-occupancy model such as voxels represents volume cells.
  • Viewing pipeline: object/model coordinates are transformed to world coordinates, then camera/view coordinates, projection coordinates and finally viewport/device coordinates. Clipping removes portions outside the viewing region before rasterization.

Exam distinction: wireframe shows edges only; surface modelling describes the exterior; solid modelling also represents the object's interior/volume. Translation, scaling and rotation are geometric transformations, while clipping determines which parts are visible in the viewing region.
