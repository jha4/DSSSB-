E-Commerce

E-commerce meaning, scope and process

Electronic commerce (e-commerce) is the conduct of commercial transactions—buying, selling, ordering, marketing, servicing and exchanging business information—through electronic networks, mainly the Internet. It includes more than the payment step: product discovery, order placement, payment, fulfilment, delivery and after-sales service.

Commerce is the wider activity of buying and selling goods and services, often on a large scale. E-commerce is commerce conducted electronically. An online catalogue without online ordering may be e-business activity, but a complete e-commerce transaction normally includes electronic order processing.

  • Pre-sale: product search, comparison, advertising, enquiry and negotiation.
  • Execution: order placement, confirmation, payment authorisation and invoice.
  • Post-order: packing, dispatch, delivery, returns, warranty and customer support.

Delivery fulfilment includes packing, loading, shipping and delivery. Billing/invoicing records the amount due; contract conclusion is the agreement stage; service support occurs after the sale.

E-commerce models and stakeholders

Models are classified by the parties transacting: B2B (business to business), B2C (business to consumer), C2C (consumer to consumer) and C2B (consumer to business). A marketplace such as OLX is commonly a C2C example when individuals sell to other individuals. A platform can support more than one model, so classify the actual transaction rather than the brand alone.

In B2B, “B” denotes a business organisation/producer; in B2C, the seller is a business and the buyer is an individual consumer. Stakeholders can include buyers, sellers, platform operators, payment providers, logistics partners, banks and regulators.

  • Subscription model: customer pays a recurring fixed fee (monthly, quarterly or annually) for continued access to a service or content.
  • Marketplace model: platform connects buyers and sellers and may earn commission, listing or service fees.
  • Affiliate marketing: publisher refers customers to a merchant and receives commission for qualifying actions.

Electronic payment, EDI, privacy and security

Electronic Funds Transfer (EFT) is the transfer of money between accounts through electronic systems. In India, NEFT is an RBI-operated electronic funds transfer system. Cards, smart cards, mobile banking, UPI and e-wallets are payment channels/instruments; they are not interchangeable terms.

  • Smart card: plastic card containing an embedded integrated-circuit chip that can store/process data securely. A magnetic-stripe card stores data differently.
  • E-wallet: digital service that stores payment credentials and/or a stored balance and facilitates electronic payments.
  • EDI (Electronic Data Interchange): computer-to-computer exchange of standardised business documents (such as purchase orders and invoices) in an agreed structured format. It is not merely sending an ordinary email attachment.

Key risks include phishing, identity theft, unauthorised access, payment fraud, data leakage, repudiation and privacy misuse. Controls include strong authentication, encryption in transit, access control, transaction alerts, secure payment gateways, audit trails and dispute handling. Website visibility refers to how easily users can discover a site through search engines, directories, links and other channels; search-engine optimisation improves discoverability but does not itself guarantee sales.

Business and Accounting

Business organisation, activity and management

Business is an organised economic activity involving production or exchange of goods/services with the objective of earning income or profit while meeting customer needs. Trade is the buying and selling of goods/services and is a central part of business. Business activities are often grouped into industry (production/processing) and commerce (trade and auxiliaries to trade).

  • Primary activities: extraction and collection of natural resources (agriculture, mining, fishing, forestry).
  • Secondary activities: manufacturing and construction, converting raw materials into finished/semi-finished products.
  • Tertiary activities: services such as transport, banking, insurance, warehousing, communication and trade.

Sole proprietorship is owned and controlled by one person; it is easy to form but the proprietor generally has unlimited liability. Partnership is a relationship between persons who agree to share profits of a business carried on by all or any acting for all; ordinary partners generally have unlimited liability. A company is a separate legal entity and shareholders generally enjoy limited liability. A cooperative is a member-owned organisation formed for mutual benefit.

Organisation structure defines relationships among people, work, authority and resources. Centralisation concentrates decision authority at higher levels; decentralisation distributes it. Bootstrapping means starting/growing a venture primarily using founders’ own funds, operating revenue and lean resource use rather than depending initially on large external financing.

Management functions include planning (objectives and courses of action), organising (roles/resources), staffing (people), directing/leading (guidance and motivation), coordinating (alignment) and controlling (compare actual performance with standards and correct deviations). Organisation structure should support coordination, responsibility and goals.

Business economics: demand, utility, elasticity and revenue

Demand is the quantity of a good consumers are willing and able to buy at different prices during a given period, other things being equal. A mere desire is not demand: it must be backed by purchasing power and willingness to pay (effective desire). The law of demand generally states that quantity demanded varies inversely with price, ceteris paribus.

  • Movement along demand curve: caused by a change in the good’s own price.
  • Shift of demand curve: caused by income, tastes, prices of related goods, expectations or number of buyers.
  • Normal good: demand usually rises as income rises. Inferior good: demand may fall as income rises.
  • Price elasticity of demand: percentage change in quantity demanded divided by percentage change in price (usually reported in absolute value). Perfectly elastic demand has an infinite elasticity; perfectly inelastic demand has zero elasticity.

Marginal revenue (MR) is the change in total revenue divided by the change in quantity sold: MR = ΔTR/ΔQ. Marginal utility is the additional satisfaction from consuming one more unit. The law of diminishing marginal utility says that, under stated assumptions, marginal utility tends to decline as more units of the same good are consumed.

Demand forecasting estimates future demand. Methods for new products can include opinion polling, vicarious methods, evolutionary approaches, substitute-product analysis and growth-curve approaches. These labels are textbook-specific; do not treat “growth curve” and “substitute approach” as synonyms.

Economy, inflation and banking

Microeconomics studies the economic behaviour of individual units such as a consumer, household or firm. Macroeconomics studies aggregates such as national income, general price level, employment and overall output. A market economy relies substantially on price signals and private decisions; a command/socialist economy gives the state a larger role in resource allocation. Real economies can be mixed.

  • Demand-pull inflation: general prices rise because aggregate demand grows faster than the economy’s ability to supply.
  • Cost-push inflation: prices rise because production costs (such as wages or raw materials) increase.
  • Working capital: funds required for day-to-day operations/current assets. Fixed capital: funds invested in long-lived assets such as plant and machinery.

Reserve Bank of India (RBI) is India’s central bank. The Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) is the prescribed share of a bank’s net demand and time liabilities maintained as cash balance with the RBI. The Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR) is maintained by banks themselves in specified liquid assets. Open-market operations (OMO) are RBI purchases/sales of government securities: an RBI sale generally absorbs liquidity and reduces banking-system reserves; a purchase injects liquidity. Distinguish CRR from SLR and policy rates from reserve requirements.

Financial accounting: meaning, principles and accounting cycle

Accounting is the process of identifying, measuring, recording, classifying, summarising, interpreting and communicating financial information about an entity. It supports stewardship, decision-making and assessment of financial performance/position. Transactions are recorded in monetary terms and follow the applicable accounting framework.

The accounting equation is Assets = Liabilities + Owner’s Equity. Every transaction has a dual effect. Under the traditional rules: personal accounts—debit the receiver, credit the giver; real accounts—debit what comes in, credit what goes out; nominal accounts—debit expenses/losses, credit incomes/gains. Under the modern approach, assets and expenses normally increase by debit; liabilities, equity and income normally increase by credit.

  1. Identify and analyse transaction with source document.
  2. Record chronologically in the journal (book of original entry).
  3. Post entries to the ledger (account-wise classification).
  4. Prepare trial balance to check arithmetical equality of debits and credits.
  5. Pass adjustment entries and prepare adjusted trial balance.
  6. Prepare financial statements (trading account, profit and loss account and balance sheet, as applicable).
  7. Close temporary nominal accounts and carry forward permanent balances.

Permanent accounts (assets, liabilities and capital) carry balances into the next period. Temporary accounts (income, expenses and drawings/dividends as applicable) are closed at period end. A trial balance may agree even when errors of omission, principle, compensating errors or complete reversal have occurred.

Trading account, profit and loss, balance sheet and stock

The Trading Account determines gross profit or gross loss from core trading activity. In simplified form: Gross Profit = Net Sales − Cost of Goods Sold (COGS); COGS = Opening Inventory + Net Purchases + Direct Expenses − Closing Inventory. If gross profit is 20% on sales, then GP = 0.20 × Sales and COGS = 0.80 × Sales. Do not confuse “20% on sales” with “20% on cost”.

The Profit and Loss Account starts with gross profit and deducts indirect expenses while adding other incomes to determine net profit/loss. The Balance Sheet reports assets, liabilities and equity at a point in time. Rent received in advance is not income earned in the current period; it is generally shown as a liability (unearned income) while only the earned portion is credited to current-period income.

When stock is destroyed by fire, estimate stock at the date of loss using the trading account/stock estimation method, then deduct salvage value to determine the stock loss. Insurance claims depend on the policy and evidence. Working steps: calculate cost of goods available, estimate cost of sales using the gross-profit rate, derive stock at the loss date, then subtract salvage.

Depreciation, reserves, provisions and long-term assets

Depreciation is the systematic allocation of the depreciable amount of a tangible fixed asset over its useful life. It reflects consumption of economic benefits, not necessarily a market-value decline. It is charged over time and reduces the asset’s carrying amount (directly or through accumulated depreciation).

  • Straight-line method: annual depreciation = (cost − residual value) ÷ useful life; equal charge each full year.
  • Written-down/reducing-balance method: depreciation = fixed rate × opening carrying amount; charge usually declines over time.
  • Accumulated depreciation: total depreciation recognised to date.
  • Book/carrying value: cost less accumulated depreciation and impairment.

A provision is recognised for a present obligation or expected loss/expense when recognition requirements are met and amount/timing is uncertain. A reserve generally represents retained/appropriated profit or an equity component, not the same as a provision. Fixed assets are long-term resources held for use in operations; intangible assets (such as patents) can also be non-current assets. Current liabilities are obligations expected to be settled in the operating cycle or within the applicable short-term period.

Knowledge Management

Knowledge, data, information and knowledge types

Data are raw facts or observations. Information is data processed and placed in context so that it is meaningful. Knowledge combines information with experience, interpretation, skills and judgement so that a person or organisation can act.

  • Tacit knowledge: personal, context-specific know-how built through experience, intuition and practice; difficult to articulate, codify or transfer completely (e.g. a technician’s diagnostic judgement).
  • Explicit knowledge: knowledge expressed in words, numbers or symbols and stored in documents, manuals, databases, procedures and videos.
  • Implicit knowledge: not yet documented but potentially articulable; the term is sometimes used inconsistently, so distinguish it from fully tacit and already codified explicit knowledge.

Shared experience can create common know-how; knowledge is not merely stored information. “Best practices” are methods regarded as effective solutions in an organisation or industry. Knowledge management is the broader discipline that identifies, develops, captures, organises, shares and applies such knowledge.

Knowledge management process and KMS

Knowledge Management (KM) is the systematic process of creating/acquiring, capturing, organising, storing, sharing, transferring and applying knowledge to achieve organisational objectives. It connects people, processes, culture and technology; it is not just buying software or building a database.

  1. Creation/acquisition: generate new knowledge or obtain it from employees, customers, partners, research and external sources.
  2. Capture/codification: document lessons, expertise, procedures and insights so they can be reused.
  3. Organisation/refinement: classify, tag, connect and add value by identifying relationships, synthesising and abstracting.
  4. Storage: preserve knowledge in repositories, document systems, databases and knowledge bases.
  5. Sharing/distribution: make relevant knowledge available through communities, collaboration, training and search.
  6. Application: use knowledge in decisions, problem solving, innovation and daily work; evaluate outcomes and update knowledge.

A Knowledge Management System (KMS) is a socio-technical system supporting these activities. It may include document/repository tools, search, collaboration platforms, expert directories, lessons-learned databases, workflow and analytics. An off-the-shelf solution may have lower initial development cost than building in-house, but total cost must include licensing, configuration, integration, security, training and maintenance. A KMS should serve strategy, sharing, retrieval and reuse; portability alone is not its central purpose.

Information systems, MIS, DSS and expert systems

An information system is a set of interrelated components that collect/retrieve, process, store and distribute information to support decision-making, coordination and control. Typical components include people, procedures/processes, data, software, hardware and networks.

  • Office Automation System (OAS): supports routine office work such as word processing, electronic filing, email, messaging, data storage and voice/data communication.
  • Management Information System (MIS): converts operational data into routine, structured reports for planning, monitoring and control. It serves management levels and functional areas; sources can include internal and external databases.
  • Decision Support System (DSS): interactive computer-based support for semi-structured/unstructured decisions using data, models and an interface. It improves decision capability; it does not replace the decision-maker.
  • Expert system: applies specialised domain knowledge and inference rules to solve problems in a narrow domain. Core elements include knowledge base, inference engine, user interface and knowledge acquisition/engineering.

Information quality dimensions can include accuracy, relevance, timeliness, completeness, accessibility, interpretability, format, coherence and compatibility. Strategic information concerns long-term objectives and policy; tactical information supports middle-management resource allocation; operational information supports day-to-day execution.

Do not confuse MIS with KMS: MIS primarily supplies information for management and control; KMS captures, shares and applies organisational knowledge. DSS is a decision-support application; an expert system encodes specialised expert reasoning. The terms overlap in modern systems but are not synonyms.

Numerical Techniques

Descriptive statistics and measures

Statistics concerns collecting, organising, presenting, analysing and interpreting data. A population is the complete group of interest; a sample is a subset. A frequency distribution summarises how often values or classes occur.

  • Mean: arithmetic average, \(\bar{x}=\sum x_i/n\). For frequency data, \(\bar{x}=\sum f_ix_i/\sum f_i\).
  • Median: middle observation after sorting; for even n, average the two middle observations.
  • Mode: most frequently occurring value.
  • Range: maximum − minimum.
  • Variance: average squared deviation from the mean. Population variance divides by N; sample variance commonly divides by n−1.
  • Standard deviation: square root of variance; expressed in the original unit.

The algebraic sum of deviations from the arithmetic mean is zero: \(\sum(x_i-\bar{x})=0\). This property is not generally true when deviations are measured from the median, mode, geometric mean or harmonic mean.

Probability, conditional probability and distributions

For equally likely outcomes, \(P(A)=\frac{\text{favourable outcomes}}{\text{total outcomes}}\), with \(0\le P(A)\le1\). The complement rule is \(P(A')=1-P(A)\). The addition rule is \(P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)\).

  • Conditional probability: \(P(A\mid B)=P(A\cap B)/P(B)\), for P(B)>0.
  • Independent events: \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)\). Mutually exclusive events with positive probabilities are not independent.
  • Bayes’ theorem: \(P(A_i\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid A_i)P(A_i)}{\sum_jP(B\mid A_j)P(A_j)}\).
  • Bernoulli: one trial with success/failure. Binomial: fixed number of independent trials with constant success probability.
  • Poisson: discrete distribution for event counts under suitable independence/constant-rate assumptions; parameter λ is both mean and variance.
  • Normal: continuous, symmetric bell-shaped distribution; mean = median = mode.

For a Bernoulli variable with success probability p, variance is p(1−p). For independent attempts with success probabilities p₁, p₂, …, the probability of at least one success is 1−∏(1−pᵢ). A contingency-table test for association between two categorical variables commonly uses the chi-square test of independence, subject to expected-frequency assumptions.

Matrices, determinants and eigenvalue basics

A matrix is a rectangular array of numbers. A matrix with m rows and n columns has order m×n. Addition requires equal orders; multiplication AB is defined when columns of A equal rows of B. Matrix multiplication is associative and distributive but generally not commutative.

  • Identity matrix: diagonal entries 1 and other entries 0; AI=IA=A.
  • Transpose: rows and columns are interchanged.
  • Determinant: for \(\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}\), determinant is ad−bc.
  • Inverse: a square matrix is invertible iff its determinant is non-zero.
  • Characteristic equation: \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\); its roots are eigenvalues.

For powers of matrices, use structure (diagonal/triangular/idempotent/involutory forms) rather than expanding blindly. A matrix is idempotent if A²=A and involutory if A²=I. A sparse matrix contains a high proportion of zero entries; a dense matrix contains comparatively few zeros.

Numerical errors, approximation and root finding

Numerical analysis develops algorithms for approximate solutions when exact analytical solutions are difficult or unsuitable for computation. Results should be assessed for accuracy, stability and convergence.

  • Absolute error: \(|x_{true}-x_{approx}|\).
  • Relative error: absolute error divided by \(|x_{true}|\), when true value is non-zero.
  • Percentage error: relative error × 100%.
  • Round-off error: caused by finite precision/rounding in representation or arithmetic.
  • Truncation error: caused by replacing an infinite process or exact expression with a finite approximation.

Bisection method: requires a continuous function and an interval [a,b] with a sign change; repeatedly halves the bracket, guaranteeing a bracketed root under these conditions. Newton–Raphson: \(x_{n+1}=x_n-f(x_n)/f'(x_n)\); often fast near a simple root but depends on a good starting value and non-zero derivative, and can diverge.

Stopping criteria may use interval width, successive-iterate difference or residual |f(x)|. Do not report more significant digits than the input precision justifies.

Interpolation and finite differences

Interpolation estimates an unknown function value within the range of given tabulated data. Extrapolation estimates outside the known range and is usually less reliable. Interpolation assumes the data can be represented adequately by a suitable interpolating function.

  • Lagrange interpolation: constructs a polynomial through known points \((x_i,y_i)\): \(P(x)=\sum_i y_iL_i(x)\), where \(L_i(x)=\prod_{j\ne i}\frac{x-x_j}{x_i-x_j}\). It works for distinct nodes, including unequally spaced x-values.
  • Newton divided differences: convenient for unequally spaced nodes; uses divided-difference coefficients.
  • Newton forward difference: suited to equally spaced data and values near the beginning of the table.
  • Newton backward difference: suited to equally spaced data and values near the end of the table.
  • Finite differences: forward, backward and central differences estimate changes/derivatives from tabulated values.

Polynomial interpolation uses one polynomial across the data set and may oscillate for high degree. Piecewise linear interpolation joins adjacent points by straight segments. Spline interpolation uses piecewise low-degree polynomials joined with continuity/smoothness conditions; cubic splines are common and often produce smoother interpolants than a single high-degree polynomial. Curve fitting/regression models an overall trend and generally does not require the fitted curve to pass through every observation, unlike interpolation.
