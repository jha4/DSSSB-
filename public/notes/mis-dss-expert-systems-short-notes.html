1. Information systems: meaning and components

Information system (IS) is an organized combination of people, procedures, data and technology that collects, processes, stores and distributes information to support operations, coordination, control and decision-making.

Data are raw facts; information is data processed and organized so it has meaning for a user and purpose. Knowledge is interpreted information combined with experience, rules or understanding that can guide action.

ComponentFunction
PeopleUsers, managers, analysts, operators and IT staff who develop or use the system.
HardwareComputers, servers, devices, storage and network equipment.
SoftwareOperating systems, DBMS, applications and supporting programs.
DataFacts and records processed into useful information.
ProceduresRules and methods for collecting, processing, securing and using information.
Networks / telecommunicationsConnect systems and enable data exchange; often treated as part of the technology resources.

An information system is not merely a computer or software package. People, procedures and organizational context are essential components.

Quick distinction: TPS records transactions; OAS supports office work; MIS produces management reports; DSS supports analytical decisions; ESS supports senior strategic monitoring; an expert system applies specialist knowledge to a domain problem.

3. Management Information System (MIS)

Management Information System is a planned and integrated system for collecting, processing, storing and disseminating information required by managers to perform planning, organizing, directing, coordinating and controlling functions.

How MIS works

  1. Input: collects internal data from TPS and operational departments, and external data such as market, economic or competitor information.
  2. Processing: validates, classifies, summarizes, compares and analyzes the data.
  3. Storage: maintains organized records and historical information in databases or data stores.
  4. Output: produces routine, summary, exception and ad hoc reports, dashboards or alerts.
  5. Feedback and control: managers compare actual performance with plans/standards and initiate corrective action.

Internal information: sales, inventory, production, finance and personnel records. External information: market trends, competitors, regulations, suppliers and economic indicators. MIS combines relevant sources to support managerial work.

Common MIS examples: railway reservation information, electronic banking operations, logistics management, inventory reporting and sales performance systems. Whether a system is called MIS or TPS depends on the function being discussed: the transaction-capture part is TPS; management reporting and analysis built on those records is MIS.

Levels of management information

LevelTime horizon / natureTypical decision
StrategicLong-term; broad, future-oriented and often unstructured.Set organizational objectives, policy and long-term direction.
TacticalMedium-term; supports departmental plans and resource allocation.Prepare budgets, staffing plans and quarterly targets.
OperationalShort-term/day-to-day; detailed and routine.Schedule shifts, process orders and monitor daily stock.

Information quality

  • Intrinsic quality: information is accurate, objective, believable and reputable.
  • Contextual quality: information is relevant, timely, complete and appropriate to the task.
  • Representational quality: information is clear, interpretable, consistently formatted and compatible with how it is used.
  • Accessibility quality: information is obtainable when needed, while access remains appropriately controlled and secure.

Exam cue: interpretability, format, coherence and compatibility are representational-quality characteristics. Relevance is contextual; accuracy is intrinsic; ease of access is accessibility.

4. Decision Support System (DSS)

Decision Support System is an interactive computer-based system that helps a decision-maker analyze data and models to evaluate alternatives, especially for semi-structured and unstructured problems. It supports human judgement; it does not automatically replace the manager or guarantee the best decision.

ComponentFunctionExample
User interface / dialog subsystemAllows the user to enter data/assumptions, run models and view results.Dashboard, forms, charts and scenario controls.
Database / data-management subsystemStores and retrieves internal and external data.Sales, inventory, costs and market information.
Model base / model-management subsystemStores and executes analytical models.Forecasting, simulation, optimization and statistical models.
Knowledge base (where included)Stores rules, constraints or domain knowledge used to guide analysis.Business rules and decision guidelines.

Some textbooks present the three core DSS subsystems as database, model base and user interface; other frameworks include a knowledge-based subsystem. Match the component list to the wording of the question.

  • What-if analysis: changes one or more assumptions and observes the effect on outcomes.
  • Sensitivity analysis: studies how changes in selected inputs affect a result.
  • Goal-seeking: starts with a desired result and works backward to determine the input needed to reach it.
  • Simulation: models the behaviour of a system under different conditions or scenarios.
  • Optimization: searches for the best solution under stated objectives and constraints.

MIS vs DSS: MIS generally provides routine, structured reports; DSS provides interactive analysis and models for less structured decisions. Both may use the same underlying organizational data.

5. Expert systems

Expert system is an AI-based program that emulates the decision-making ability of a human specialist within a limited domain. It represents domain knowledge explicitly and applies an inference mechanism to facts supplied by a user or other system.

ComponentRole
Knowledge baseStores domain facts, rules, heuristics and relationships.
Inference engineApplies logical rules to known facts to derive conclusions or recommendations.
User interfaceCollects user inputs and presents advice, questions and results.
Explanation facilityExplains why the system asked a question or reached a conclusion.
Knowledge acquisition moduleHelps capture, encode and update specialist knowledge.

Knowledge engineer: person who works with domain experts to elicit, structure, encode and maintain knowledge in the system. The domain expert supplies specialist knowledge; the knowledge engineer helps represent it in a usable form.

  • Forward chaining: data-driven reasoning; starts from known facts and applies rules to derive new facts or conclusions.
  • Backward chaining: goal-driven reasoning; starts with a hypothesis or goal and works backward to determine which facts/rules would support it.
  • Narrow domain: expert systems are specialized; they do not have general human-level understanding.
  • Advantages: consistent advice, availability, preservation of scarce expertise and explanation of rule-based conclusions.
  • Limitations: knowledge acquisition is difficult, rules can become outdated, exceptions may be missed and outputs depend on knowledge quality.

Do not confuse: an expert system's inference engine applies rules in its knowledge base. An MIS typically analyzes operational/external data to produce management information; those are different system roles.

6. OLTP and batch processing

OLTP (Online Transaction Processing) processes operational transactions as they occur, usually through many short, concurrent interactions. It emphasizes fast response, transaction integrity, consistency, availability and an up-to-date operational database.

  • Examples: ATM withdrawals, ticket booking, point-of-sale purchases, order entry and bank transfers.
  • Typical properties: high transaction volume, short transactions, concurrent users, frequent insert/update/delete operations and strong consistency requirements.
  • ACID properties are commonly associated with reliable database transactions: Atomicity, Consistency, Isolation and Durability.

Batch processing collects a set of transactions or jobs and processes them together at a scheduled time or when a batch is ready. Examples include payroll, periodic billing and bulk report generation.

OLTPBatch processing

OLTP vs OLAP: OLTP supports day-to-day transaction processing; OLAP (Online Analytical Processing) supports multidimensional analysis of large amounts of historical or summarized data for decision-making. A data warehouse is commonly used for analytical workloads, not as a replacement for the live transaction system.

7. Knowledge Management and Knowledge Management Systems

Knowledge Management (KM) is the systematic process of identifying, creating/acquiring, organizing, storing, sharing and applying knowledge within an organization.

  • Tacit knowledge: personal, experience-based know-how that is difficult to write down fully, such as judgement developed through practice.
  • Explicit knowledge: knowledge that has been documented and can be stored or transferred, such as manuals, policies, reports and databases.
  • KMS (Knowledge Management System): technology and processes that support knowledge capture, classification, storage, search, sharing, reuse and learning.
  • Knowledge lifecycle: creation/acquisition → codification/organization → storage → distribution/sharing → application → review and updating.

KM is broader than storing documents: it also concerns people, culture, processes and reuse of organizational learning. Expert systems can encode explicit specialist rules, while tacit expertise must first be elicited and represented.
